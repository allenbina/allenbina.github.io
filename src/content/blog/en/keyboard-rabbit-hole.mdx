It started with dad wrist.

My daughter was about 8 months old and I was carrying her constantly. My wrists were killing me, especially at the keyboard after a long day of holding a baby. I got a Logitech ergonomic keyboard thinking that would fix it.

It helped. But then I went down the rabbit hole.

## The Dactyl Manuform

I built the [Dactyl Manuform](/projects/keyboard) from scratch — 3D printed the case, hand-wired every switch, soldered in the diodes, flashed the firmware. It was my first big hardware project and I documented the whole thing. Started with KMK firmware (Python-based), eventually moved to Vial QMK for better features.

The printing alone was an ordeal. Each body was a 3+ day print. My first test print broke the filament somehow and stopped within 3 hours. I had prints shift mid-job, temperature issues, you name it. I went through a dozen test prints just for the microcontroller holders and couldn't figure out why the dimensions were off. Printed calibration cubes, printed what I kept calling a "calibration cauliflower" — tried everything. Eventually I nearly took the whole printer apart and put it back together. That's when I found the belts weren't tensioned properly. Tightened them up, problem solved.

![3D printer running a test print for the keyboard case](/images/blog/keyboard-rabbit-hole/IMG_4032_3dprint.jpg)

The real prints — the actual keyboard bodies — took 3+ days each. I set up OctoPrint so I could check on them at night from my phone. I was convinced they would fail. I stood in amazement both times when they actually finished.

![Hand-wired switch matrix during the build](/images/blog/keyboard-rabbit-hole/IMG_4287.jpg)

It was an incredible learning experience. But it was also massive. Two halves the size of softballs sitting on my desk.

## The Silakka 54

Got the Silakka 54 for work. It lives at my desk at the coworking space with a USB switcher to go between my work laptop and personal laptop — like a poor man's KVM switch. I tented it with phone stands where I removed one of the arms to make them shorter. Janky but it works.

## The Corne

Went smaller. The Corne drops the number row entirely — everything is done through layers and combos. It's a beautiful, minimal board. But the combos took a lot of getting used to, and I missed the number row more than I expected.

## The Sofle

I actually bought a DIY Sofle kit first. Got about halfway through the build and realized I'd much rather just pay for a finished one. After the Dactyl I'd proven I could do it — I didn't need to prove it again. So I bought a pre-built Sofle. It has the number row back, plus rotary encoders, and it's a good middle ground between the Corne's minimalism and the Dactyl's bulk. This is the one I travel with, though honestly I use the laptop keyboard most of the time. My muscle memory goes back and forth pretty easily.

![Sofle DIY kit mid-build](/images/blog/keyboard-rabbit-hole/IMG_5193_sofle_diy.jpg)

The Sofle and the Corne are my "eyelash" keyboards — the travel ones. They click and clack a lot though. I have brown switches so they don't have the tactile click, but they just thud in the plastic. I'm told dampeners would help but I'm also a dad, so time is at a premium.

![The eyelash Sofle — compact travel keyboard](/images/blog/keyboard-rabbit-hole/IMG_6134_eyelash_sofle.jpg)

## Am I Done?

If I had endless money, I'd probably look at a Dygma Raise or a Glove80 — though I hear the tenting is bad on the Glove80. But if I'm being real, I'm probably not getting another keyboard. The Sofle does what I need. The Silakka lives at work. The Dactyl proved I could build one from scratch.

That's enough keyboards.
