I was knee deep in IR blasters for a while.

It started simple — I wanted to make my TV smart. Send power on, change inputs, adjust volume, all from Home Assistant. I built a transmitter with an ESP8266 and an IR LED, pointed it at the TV, and cloned the remote codes.

![Early IR blaster build — ESP8266 with IR LED](/images/blog/ir-blasters/IMG_2141_ir_blaster_early.jpg)

But then I realized I could also control the AC in the bedroom. Same principle — capture the remote codes, replay them from an ESP. The AC remotes are more complex than TV remotes (they send the entire state — temp, mode, fan speed — in every burst), but once you decode the protocol it's straightforward.

![IR blaster mounted for AC control](/images/blog/ir-blasters/PXL_ir_blaster_ac_installed.jpg)

Then it got more interesting. I started playing with IR *receive* in a separate area, so I could use the TV remote to trigger Home Assistant automations. Point the remote at a sensor, press a button, and it fires a webhook. Turn on the AC from the couch. Toggle lights. Whatever you want.

I had so many different ones at one point. Different form factors, different ESP boards, different IR LED arrangements. Some with a single LED for targeted control, some with multiple LEDs for wide-angle coverage. I was building them faster than I had things to point them at.

![Later IR blaster — cleaner build pointed at the TV](/images/blog/ir-blasters/IMG_0372_ir_blaster_tv.jpg)

## The Case Problem

At some point I wanted actual enclosures for these things instead of bare circuit boards taped to the wall. I didn't have a 3D printer yet (that would come a couple years later), so I tried bending plastic with a heat gun.

Nobody tells you this: plastic has a reasonably high melting point. Much higher than the pain point of your finger. I heated a piece of acrylic until it softened, went to bend it by hand, and immediately blistered my finger. Lesson learned.

The real problem was that doing this in an apartment with a cheap heat gun and no proper forms was never going to produce clean results. I wished I had a woodshop where I could make wooden shells to bend the plastic around. Instead I got wobbly, uneven boxes that kind of fit but looked terrible. A 3D printer solved this problem completely — but I didn't know that yet.
