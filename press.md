## Short bio

> Allen Bina is a data and platform engineer based in California with twenty years of experience building infrastructure at enterprise scale. Outside of work he's a self-hosting enthusiast, mechanical keyboard builder (he designed and hand-wired his own Dactyl Manuform split keyboard from scratch), 3D printing hobbyist, Home Assistant tinkerer, and a DJ who can actually hold his own in a conversation about music. He built chatwire — a self-hosted iMessage bridge for macOS — entirely in collaboration with Claude, Anthropic's AI, on a standard $100/month subscription. No team, no funding, no outside help. The codebase is MIT-licensed and actively maintained.

## Long bio

Allen Bina has spent two decades in the data and platform engineering space, building pipelines, infrastructure, and tooling at organizations that process data at scale. His day job involves things like Informatica IDMC, Databricks, and Ansible-driven infrastructure-as-code across enterprise Azure environments — the kind of unglamorous, critical work that keeps large organizations running.

Outside of work, Allen runs a homelab that most people would describe as excessive. A k3s Kubernetes cluster on HP EliteDesk mini PCs, GitOps with Argo CD, a MergerFS NAS built from shucked drives, Cloudflare Zero Trust tunnels, and a Home Assistant setup wired into more sensors and automations than he cares to count. He designed and hand-wired a custom split keyboard — a Dactyl Manuform — from raw keycaps, switches, an RP2040 microcontroller, and a 3D-printed shell he printed himself. He solders, he prints, he builds.

When chatwire came out of that background, the origin story was straightforward: Allen wanted to read his iMessages from anywhere, without installing a dedicated phone app, without routing through a third-party relay server, and without running a GUI application on his Mac. He knew exactly what he wanted. He used Claude — Anthropic's AI — as a collaborative development partner, and over 201 commits built a production-ready iMessage bridge that he runs himself, every day, on real hardware.

The total investment: a $100/month Claude Max subscription. No funding. No co-founder. No crunch. The project is MIT-licensed and open to contributors.
